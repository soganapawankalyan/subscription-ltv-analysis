# Subscription Revenue, Churn and LTV Analysis

**Live dashboard:** https://soganapawankalyan.github.io/subscription-ltv-analysis/dashboard/

**SQL (SQLite) + Python | 25,000 synthetic customers | 36 months**

Which customers are worth acquiring, and what did a price rise and a promotion really do to revenue?
This project answers those questions for a streaming-style subscription business using SQL for the
metrics and Python for survival curves and a 5-year lifetime-value projection.

> **Disclaimer.** The business is fictional and the data is **synthetic**, generated by
> `generate_subscription_data.py`. It is inspired by subscription companies in general. It contains
> no data from, and makes no claims about, any real company or its decisions.

---

## The business question

A subscription business grows MRR (monthly recurring revenue) while customers keep leaving.
Leadership wants to know:

1. Is growth healthy, or is it only new signups covering for existing customers shrinking?
2. Which acquisition channels earn back their cost, and how fast?
3. Did the March 2025 price rise on the Standard plan make money once the extra cancellations are counted?
4. Do promotions build loyalty, or just delay churn?

## Headline findings

| # | Finding | Evidence |
|---|---------|----------|
| 1 | **MRR grew from $7.3K to $214K, but existing customers shrink.** 12-month net revenue retention (NRR) is below 100% every month (64% in Jan 2024, 78% in Dec 2025), so growth depends on new signups. | `02_mrr_bridge.sql` |
| 2 | **Paid social does not earn back its cost.** CAC is about $102 and rising ($91 to $115 over three years). Only 47.8% of customers remain after 12 months. Projected 60-month LTV:CAC is 1.7x, with payback around month 21. | `04_ltv_cac.sql`, `06_survival_ltv.py` |
| 3 | **Referral and partnership are the efficient channels.** 12-month retention is 76.8% and 71.9%; 60-month LTV:CAC is 15.3x and 8.0x; payback is in month 3 and month 5. | same |
| 4 | **The churn improvement is mostly mix shift.** Blended monthly churn fell from 4.87% (2023) to 3.55% (2025). At a fixed 2023 channel mix it is 4.87%, 3.89%, 3.94%: within channels, churn did not improve from 2024 to 2025. | `05_diagnostics.sql` (5.2) |
| 5 | **The first year decides retention.** Monthly churn is 6.1% in month 2, about 3.4% by month 12 and 2.3% after month 13. Cohort quality is stable: 12-month retention is 59 to 63% for every signup quarter. | `03_churn_retention.sql` |
| 6 | **The promotion delays churn but does not prevent it.** Promo customers churn 10.7% in month 4, when the discount ends, against 4.2% for others. By month 12 their retention is no better (59.5% vs 61.4%). | `05_diagnostics.sql` (5.3, 5.4) |
| 7 | **The price rise paid off, at a cost.** Standard churn rose from about 3.0% to 4.3 to 5.4% for three months, adding about 299 extra cancellations. Price gain was about $10.5K/month of MRR against about $4.8K/month lost to the extra churn, so roughly +$5.7K/month net, before counting the future value of those 299 customers. | `05_diagnostics.sql` (5.5, 5.6) |

## Recommendations

1. **Shift budget from paid social toward referral and partnership.** On these numbers a dollar in
   referral returns roughly nine times more lifetime profit than a dollar in paid social. Test the
   shift with a capped budget change before committing.
2. **Rework the promotion.** A 50% discount that only delays the cancellation costs revenue. Test a
   smaller discount, or one that steps up gradually, against the current design.
3. **Roll out price rises with a retention offer.** Consider a staged increase or a grandfathering
   offer for customers most likely to leave, and monitor downgrades (which also jumped, to 1.95%).
4. **Invest in the first 90 days.** Churn is highest in months 2 to 3, so onboarding is where
   retention is cheapest to improve.
5. **Test an early-upgrade nudge.** Customers who upgrade within three months retain better
   (77.8% vs 65.3% at 12 months, n = 288). See the caveat below.

## Data and what is built in

The data generator documents its assumptions in its docstring. The most important point for a reader:
**several of the patterns above were planted on purpose** (the promo cliff in month 4, the price-rise
churn response, the early-upgrade effect, the mix shift away from paid social, and the differences in
channel quality). The analysis shows the methods recover them; it does not "discover" them. On real
data the same queries would find whatever is there.

| Table | Rows | Key fields |
|-------|------|------------|
| `customers` | 25,000 | signup month, channel, country, device, signup plan, promo flag |
| `subscription_months` | 281,517 | customer, month, plan, status (active/paused), MRR, tenure month |
| `events` | 41,208 | signup, cancel, upgrade, downgrade, pause, resume |
| `marketing_spend` | 180 | month x channel spend in USD |

Prices: Basic $7.99, Standard $13.99 (to $15.99 from March 2025), Premium $19.99. Promo signups
(Nov 2023 and Nov 2024, plus a few others) pay 50% for their first three months.

## Method

- **SQL** builds an MRR bridge (new, upgrade, promo roll-off, price increase, resume, downgrade,
  pause, churn) that reconciles to actual MRR to the cent, then churn by month and tenure, cohort
  retention, CAC, LTV, payback and diagnostics.
- **Retention uses only eligible customers.** A customer counts toward 12-month retention only if
  they signed up at least 12 months before the data ends. Otherwise recent cohorts look falsely good
  (right-censoring).
- **Python** fits Kaplan-Meier survival curves (customers still subscribed at Dec 2025 are
  censored, not counted as retained forever), then projects gross-profit LTV to 60 months: the
  observed curve up to month 24, then constant churn at each channel's months 13 to 24 average.
  Customers are bootstrapped (300 resamples) for 90% intervals.

### Validation

Nine integrity checks in `01_profile_validate.sql` all return 0 problems (duplicate customer-months,
billing after cancel, gaps in history, paused-but-billed, spend missing for a signup channel, and so
on). The MRR bridge reconciles with a maximum difference of 0.0. The Python retention curves agree
with the SQL cohort tables to within about one percentage point (the Python version uses all customers,
the SQL only those old enough to reach each milestone). One bug in my own SQL (the cumulative profit
curve) was found by cross-checking two results against each other and fixed.

## Assumptions and limitations

- **Gross margin is assumed at 70%**, not observed. It scales every LTV figure but does not change
  how the channels rank.
- **About half of 60-month LTV is extrapolated** beyond the 24 months we can observe, using constant
  churn. The bootstrap intervals only capture sampling noise, not whether that assumption holds. Trust
  the channel ranking more than the exact dollar figures.
- **Organic CAC is a tracking gap.** Organic spend only includes a small tracked cost, so its LTV:CAC
  (34.9x) is overstated and should not be used as a benchmark.
- **Mix-adjusted churn controls for channel only**, not for how long customers have been subscribed.
- **Plan and channel overlap** (paid social skews toward Basic), so plan-level retention differences
  are not a pure plan effect.
- **The early-upgrade comparison is an association in real-world terms.** Engaged customers may simply
  be the ones who upgrade. (In this synthetic data an early-upgrade effect was built in; real data would
  need an experiment to establish it.)
- **The price-rise estimate** compares churn before and after the rise on the Standard plan and
  assumes the excess churn is caused by the rise. Other changes in March to May 2025 are not modelled.
- Paused customers count as subscribed (with $0 revenue); only a cancellation ends a lifetime.

## How to reproduce

```bash
python3 -m venv .venv && source .venv/bin/activate
pip install numpy pandas matplotlib
python generate_subscription_data.py     # builds data/ and data/subscriptions.db (about 20 s)
bash run_all.sh                          # runs the SQL files into results/*.txt
python 06_survival_ltv.py                # survival curves, LTV, charts, results/py_*.csv
```

Requires `sqlite3` (included with macOS). The data uses a fixed seed (42), so results are reproducible.
